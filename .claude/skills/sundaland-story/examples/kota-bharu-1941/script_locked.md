### 0:20.4 · Cold open · the clock

They're Japanese troop transports, about three kilometres offshore, carrying more than five thousand soldiers. At around half past midnight, their escorts open fire on the beach. Far across the Pacific, in Hawaii, it's dawn on Sunday. The first wave of Japanese planes is already in the air, but it won't reach Pearl Harbor for more than an hour. By most accounts, seventy-eight minutes. So the Pacific War didn't start at Pearl Harbor. It started here. And to understand why Japan came to this beach at all, you have to follow the oil.

### 0:58.8 · Act 1 · The Clock

Rewind four months. In the summer of 1941, Japan is fighting a war in China it cannot finish, and it's fighting it on borrowed fuel. Japan produces almost no oil of its own. Around eighty percent of what it imports comes from one country: the United States.

### 1:17.4 · Act 1 · The freeze

In July 1941, after Japanese troops move into southern French Indochina, Washington freezes Japanese assets. Within days, American oil stops flowing. Britain and the Dutch follow. Japan has stockpiled around fifty-four million barrels. Estimates of how long that would last range from about one year to two. Either way, a clock is now running. And every month Japan waits, its fleet gets weaker.

### 1:42.1 · Act 1 · The decision

In September, at a conference before the Emperor, Japan's leaders agree: if talks with Washington fail, they will go to war. Through the autumn, the talks go nowhere. By late November, the decision is made, and the invasion fleets are already moving.

### 1:58.8 · Act 1 · The prize

So Japan's planners look south, at Sundaland. The Dutch East Indies, today's Indonesia, is the world's fourth-largest oil exporter, with oil fields in Borneo and Sumatra. Next door, British Malaya grows something the whole industrial world runs on. In 1940, it shipped about thirty-nine percent of the world's rubber, for tyres, hoses, and every machine of war. And it's the world's biggest exporter of tin, the metal that lines food cans and solders electronics. Oil, rubber, tin. Almost everything Japan's war machine is missing sits on one shallow shelf of sea and islands.

### 2:34.8 · Act 1 · The shield

But there's a problem. Guarding the sea lanes to all of it is the British naval base at Singapore. And watching from Hawaii is the American Pacific Fleet. So Japan decides to strike everything at once. Pearl Harbor will be the shield. The target is Sundaland. And the first blow won't fall in Hawaii. It will fall on a beach in Kelantan.

### 2:59.4 · Act 1 · Why these beaches

Why Kelantan, and why Thailand? Look at the map. Singapore's defences were built mainly to face the sea. The back door is the long, narrow Malay Peninsula. Land at Songkhla and Pattani in southern Thailand, and you get the ports, the roads and the railway that run straight into northern Malaya. Land at Kota Bharu, and you take the airfield whose bombers could hit your convoys. Geography decides the plan.

### 3:26.7 · Act 2 · The convoy

In early December, the invasion convoy sails south from Hainan Island. On the seventh, an RAF Catalina flying boat finds it. Japanese aircraft shoot the Catalina down before any report gets through. Just before midnight, the convoy splits. Most of it turns toward the beaches of southern Thailand. Three transports head for Kota Bharu, near the Thai border, where the British have an airfield.

### 3:51.7 · Act 2 · The defenders

Waiting on shore are the Dogras: the third battalion of the 17th Dogra Regiment, part of the 8th Indian Brigade under Brigadier B. W. Key. They hold about sixteen kilometres of beach, with pillboxes and wire.

### 4:51.4 · Act 2 · Ashore anyway

All three transports are hit. The Awazisan Maru is left burning and abandoned. But the troops are already in the boats. By mid-morning, three Japanese battalions are ashore. And at one forty-eight, Malayan time, the first bombs fall on Pearl Harbor. By then, Kota Bharu has been under fire for more than an hour.

### 5:12.9 · Act 2 · Seven beaches

And Kota Bharu isn't alone. That same night, Japanese troops come ashore at seven places along Thailand's coast: Songkhla, Pattani, Nakhon Si Thammarat, Surat Thani, Chumphon, Prachuap Khiri Khan, and Samut Prakan, near Bangkok. Thailand is neutral, and Japan has demanded passage. But the prime minister can't be reached. While Bangkok's leaders wait for him, the landings begin.

### 5:35.6 · Act 2 · Chumphon

At Chumphon, on the narrow neck of the peninsula, the defenders include soldiers, police, and schoolboys. They're Yuwachon Thahan, youth cadets from the local secondary school. With the 38th Infantry Battalion, they take up positions at the Tha Nang Sang bridge.

### 6:16.0 · Act 3 · The race

Kota Bharu falls on the ninth of December. Then the race begins. Japan's 25th Army, under General Tomoyuki Yamashita, has to cover the length of the Malay Peninsula, about a thousand kilometres, to reach Singapore.

### 6:30.0 · Act 3 · The sea lanes

Two days later, Britain loses the sea. On the tenth of December, Japanese bombers find the battleship Prince of Wales and the battlecruiser Repulse off Kuantan, and sink them both. The ships sent to protect the route to Singapore are gone. From now on, the peninsula is a land race, and Japan is already ahead.

### 6:51.7 · Act 3 · Bicycles

And here, the prize gets in the way. Malaya's rubber estates and jungle make heavy tanks a liability. So Japan does something that sounds absurd. It rides. Around eighteen thousand bicycles carry its infantry down the roads between the rubber trees. According to one Japanese account, when tyres burst in the heat, soldiers ride on the rims, and the clatter at night is mistaken for tanks. The rubber Japan came for is, quite literally, under its wheels.

Penang is abandoned in mid-December. Kuala Lumpur falls on the eleventh of January. At the end of January, the last defenders cross the causeway onto Singapore island and blow a gap in it behind them. About a week later, Japanese troops cross the Johor Strait in the dark.

### 7:44.7 · Act 3 · Singapore

On the fifteenth of February, 1942, about seventy days after the first shells fell on Sabak beach, Singapore surrenders. It's the largest surrender in British military history. Within weeks, Japanese troops are in the Dutch East Indies, taking the oil fields of Borneo and Sumatra. Sundaland's oil, rubber and tin, the prize behind the whole southern strike, are now in Japanese hands.

### 8:09.3 · Act 3 · The twist

But holding the oil is not the same as moving it. Every barrel still has to cross the sea to Japan, past American submarines. Tanker after tanker goes down. By 1945, only a trickle of Sundaland's oil is reaching Japan. The war Japan started to escape an oil blockade ends with Japan blockaded again, at sea.

### 8:31.6 · Epilogue

The fight on the beaches at Kota Bharu cost the defenders about sixty-eight killed, and Japan more than three hundred. In Chumphon, a monument stands to the cadets, and every eighth of December, the town marks Wiratai Day, the day of Thai heroes. We remember Pearl Harbor as the beginning. But the first shots of the Pacific War were fired on a Malayan beach, in a war over oil, rubber, and tin.
